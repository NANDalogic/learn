Inside the Switch: Transistors, Logic, and Bits

What a transistor is, how chips are made, how switches become arithmetic and memory, how a keypress becomes light, how long this has been going on, and how quantum computing differs.

Recap and map

  • Transistor: a tiny switch controlled by electricity: on or off.
  • Bit: one on/off value. High voltage = 1, low voltage = 0.
  • Logic gate: a few transistors wired to follow a simple rule (AND, OR, NOT…). Everything a computer does is built from huge numbers of these rules.
The brain comparison

On the main page's matter-to-thought ladder, the transistor pairs with the ion channel (stage 3, "smallest switch"). Both are gates that open or close depending on voltage, but by different mechanisms: semiconductors and electrons versus proteins and ions. A whole neuron is far more than a gate (stage 5).

D1

The transistor: a switch with no moving parts

Silicon and doping

  • Adding a tiny amount of other atoms to silicon ("doping") changes how it conducts.[R1]
    • n-type: atoms with 5 outer electrons (e.g., phosphorus) each add a spare, free-moving electron.[R1]
    • p-type: atoms with 3 outer electrons (e.g., boron) leave a gap, a "hole", which acts like a moving positive charge.[R1]

The MOSFET, the transistor in nearly every chip

  • Parts: a source and a drain (doped silicon regions), a channel between them, and a gate (a metal electrode sitting on a very thin insulating layer, the gate oxide).[R2]
  • How it switches:
    • Put enough voltage on the gate (relative to the source), and its electric field pulls a thin layer of mobile charges into the channel just under the oxide.
    • When that layer bridges source and drain, current flows: on.
    • Bring the gate voltage near zero, and the path disappears: off.[R2]
    • The insulator means almost no current flows into the gate itself, so a small voltage controls a larger current.[R2]
  • The basic structure (gate stack, channel, source, drain) hasn't changed since the MOSFET was invented in 1959.[R2] The first device (Atalla and Kahng, Bell Labs) used a metal gate, a silicon-dioxide insulator and a silicon body.[R58]

Shapes over time: wrapping the gate around the channel

Same switch since 1959; each new shape wraps the gate further around the channel.

  1. 1959MOSFET invented. Its basic structure (gate, channel, source, drain) hasn't changed since.[R2]
  2. 2011FinFET: Intel's 22 nm "Tri-Gate". The gate covers a thin fin on 3 sides.[R3]
  3. Q4 2025Nanosheet (gate-all-around): TSMC N2 in volume production. The gate wraps stacked ribbons on all sides.[R4][R5]
  4. by Oct 2026Intel 18A: RibbonFET plus PowerVia (power from the back). Intel says it's in high-volume production.[R6]
Read the details
  • Planar: the gate sits on top of a flat channel.
  • FinFET (2011): Intel announced its 22 nm "Tri-Gate" transistor on 4 May 2011. The channel becomes a thin vertical fin with the gate on three sides.[R3] Samsung, TSMC and others followed.[R2]
  • Gate-all-around / nanosheet: the fin is replaced by a stack of thin silicon ribbons, and the gate wraps all the way around them for better control.[R4]
    • TSMC's N2 uses its first-generation nanosheet transistors and began volume production in Q4 2025.[R5]
    • Intel's 18A uses "RibbonFET" (gate-all-around) plus "PowerVia", which delivers power from the back of the chip. Intel says 18A is in high-volume production (page read Oct 2026).[R6]

  • Research frontier: Intel has reported a transistor with a 6 nm gate length and a 3 nm-thick nanosheet.[R4]

Wafers and steps

Read the details
  • Chips are built on thin, polished silicon discs called wafers. 300 mm is the size most often used.[R7]
  • ASML (the company that makes the most advanced lithography machines) lists six core steps, repeated many times:[R7]
    1. deposition (add a thin layer)
    2. photoresist coating (add a light-sensitive layer)
    3. lithography (project the pattern with light)
    4. etch (remove material where the pattern says)
    5. ion implantation (dope regions)
    6. packaging
  • Making a chip takes thousands of steps and can take more than three months from design to production.[R7]

Read the details
  • Adapted for silicon at Bell Labs in 1955 (Jules Andrus and Walter Bond), borrowing from photoengraving.[R8]
  • How it works: coat the wafer with a light-sensitive resist, shine light through a patterned mask, develop the resist, then etch or dope through the openings.[R8]
  • Shorter light wavelengths can print finer patterns:
    • DUV (deep ultraviolet): 248 nm and 193 nm lasers.[R9]
    • EUV (extreme ultraviolet): 13.5 nm light, more than 14× shorter.[R9]
      • EUV light is made by hitting molten tin droplets (about 25 µm across) with a laser twice: once to flatten each droplet, once to turn it into a glowing plasma. This happens 50,000 times a second.[R9]
      • Air absorbs EUV, so the whole light path is in a vacuum, and it uses mirrors instead of lenses.[R10]
    • Resolution: standard EUV (numerical aperture 0.33) resolves 13 nm. High-NA EUV (NA 0.55) resolves 8 nm.[R10]
Status: High-NA EUV (as of October 2026)
  • ASML shipped the first High-NA system in December 2023.[R10]
  • By early 2026, Intel, SK hynix and Samsung had High-NA tools, and the research center imec received one on 18 March 2026.[R11][R12]
  • Reuters estimates that fewer than a dozen exist, at roughly $400 million each. That's an estimate, not an ASML list price.[R11]

What "2 nm" means, and doesn't

"3 nm", "2 nm" and "18A" are generation labels, not measurements.[R13][R14]

"I can't point to the one dimension that's 32 nm or 22 nm or 14 nm." Mark Bohr, Intel [R13]
Read the details
  • Settled: node names like "3 nm", "2 nm" and "18A" are generation labels, not measurements.[R13][R14]
    • Intel's Mark Bohr: "I can't point to the one dimension that's 32 nm or 22 nm or 14 nm."[R13]
    • By the early 2000s, the node number had "nothing to do with any dimension that you can find on the die" (Paolo Gargini, former chair of the industry roadmap).[R14]
  • Real dimensions, for scale:
    • Intel's "22 nm" FinFET had a 26 nm gate length and 8 nm-wide fins.[R14]
    • The "5 nm" node has a contacted gate pitch (spacing between gates) of 48 nm and a metal pitch of 36 nm.[R14]
    • Intel 18A (marketed as 1.8 nm-class) has a contacted gate pitch of 50 nm, more than 25× the name.[R15]
  • Industry veterans have proposed describing chips by real numbers instead: gate pitch, metal pitch, and number of stacked layers of devices.[R14]

D3

From transistors to gates

Two kinds of transistor, working in pairs (CMOS)

  • Modern chips use CMOS: every gate has a pull-up network of PMOS transistors (connects the output to 1) and a pull-down network of NMOS transistors (connects the output to 0).[R16]
  • The two networks are mirror images: transistors in series in one are in parallel in the other.[R16]
  • A single CMOS stage always inverts: a high input pulls the output low.[R16]
    • So the natural one-stage gates are NOT, NAND and NOR.
    • AND and OR need a second stage: a NAND or NOR followed by a NOT.[R19][R20]

"Are transistors always grouped in 4?"

No. But the idea has two real roots:

  1. The two most basic gates, NAND and NOR, are exactly 4 transistors each.[R17][R18]
  2. The classic logic chip, the 7400 family, puts four NAND gates in one package. TI's SN74HC00 is literally named "Quadruple 2-Input NAND Gates."[R24]

Counter-examples: NOT is 2, AND/OR are 6, an SRAM cell is 6, XOR is 6–12.

Read the details
  • In plain words:
    • AND: 1 only if both inputs are 1.
    • OR: 1 if either is.
    • NAND / NOR: the opposite of AND / OR.
    • XOR ("exclusive or"): 1 if the inputs differ.
    • XNOR: 1 if they're the same.
  • How many 2-input gates are possible? 16. A 2-input table has 4 rows, and each row's output can be 0 or 1, so there are 2⁴ = 16 possible tables. Logicians count the same 16 "binary connectives".[R26] Only some have common names. Others include "always 0", "always 1", "just A", and "A AND NOT B".
  • NAND can build everything. Any logic function can be made from NAND gates alone, and the same goes for NOR.[R20][R25] For example:
    • NOT A = A NAND A
    • A AND B = NOT (A NAND B)
    • A OR B = (NOT A) NAND (NOT B)
  • History:
    • Charles Sanders Peirce discovered this around 1880, but it was only published in 1933.[R26]
    • Henry Sheffer published it in 1913, though his "stroke" meant NOR.[R26][R27]
    • Jean Nicod used it for NAND in 1917, the meaning used today.[R27]

Remembering with gates

  • Feed two gates' outputs back into each other, and the circuit has two stable states: it holds one bit.[R29]
    • Claude Shannon, 1948: "A device with two stable positions, such as a relay or a flip-flop circuit, can store one bit of information."[R30]
  • SR latch: two cross-coupled NOR (or NAND) gates. "Set" stores a 1 and "Reset" stores a 0. Pressing both at once is forbidden.[R29]
  • D latch / flip-flop: stores whatever is on the data input. A latch passes the input through while enabled; a flip-flop grabs it only on a clock tick.[R29]

D6

Bits and bytes

A byte is 8 bits, so it holds 2⁸ = 256 values (0–255). The playground below shows how.[R33]

  1. 1948"Bit" first in print, in Shannon's paper, credited to John Tukey.[R30]
  2. 1956"Byte" coined by Werner Buchholz at IBM, from "bite". 8-bit bytes adopted for Stretch.[R32]
  3. 1963ASCII: 7-bit code, 128 characters. "A" = 65.[R51]
  4. mid-1960sIBM System/360 spreads the 8-bit byte "far and wide".[R32]
  5. Sep 2026Unicode 18.0: 172,808 characters; its first 128 match ASCII.[R52][R41]
Read the details
  • Bit = binary digit, one 0 or 1. The word first appeared in print in Claude Shannon's 1948 paper, which credits it to John Tukey: "binary digits, or more briefly bits, a word suggested by J. W. Tukey."[R30]
  • Byte: coined by Werner Buchholz at IBM in 1956 while designing the Stretch computer. It was "coined from 'bite', but respelled to avoid accidental mutation to 'bit'."[R32]
    • Early bytes ranged from 1 to 6 bits. 8-bit bytes were adopted for Stretch later in 1956.[R32]
    • IBM's System/360 spread the 8-bit byte "far and wide in the mid-1960s."[R32] Bytes from 1 to 48 bits have been used; "octet" means exactly 8.[R33]
  • One byte holds 2⁸ = 256 values (0–255).[R33]
  • How bits become numbers (place value): each position is worth double the one to its right: 128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1. Add up the positions holding a 1.[R34]
    • Example: 0100 0001 = 64 + 1 = 65.
  • Hexadecimal (base 16, digits 0–9 and A–F) is shorthand: one hex digit = 4 bits, so a byte is two hex digits, 00 to FF.[R35] 65 = 0x41.

Keypress-to-pixel pipeline

A letter on screen is a chain of codes and transistors from finger to light.

This demo needs JavaScript. All 12 steps are listed below.

What this leaves outThis shows a USB keyboard on Windows; other systems differ in details. Real steps overlap and take microseconds to milliseconds; no timings are shown, since none are sourced.
All 12 steps as text
  1. Key switch → circuit. Keys sit on a grid of row and column wires. Pressing a key connects one row to one column.[R36]
  2. The keyboard scans. Its tiny processor energizes the columns one at a time and reads all the rows, many times a second, to see which junctions are connected. Diodes stop false "ghost" presses.[R36]
  3. The keyboard sends a code, not a letter. It reports which key is down, not what character it means.[R37]
  4. Over USB: a standard keyboard report is 8 bytes: one for modifier keys (Shift, Ctrl…), one reserved, and up to six key codes.[R38] The "A" key is usage 0x04, and Left Shift is 0xE1.[R39]
  5. The OS applies your keyboard layout. The operating system turns the key code into a character, taking Shift and Caps Lock into account. The layout decides which character each key produces.[R40]
  6. The character code: "A" is Unicode U+0041, "LATIN CAPITAL LETTER A".[R41] That's decimal 65, binary 0100 0001. In UTF-8 (the web's standard encoding), it's stored as that single byte.[R42]
  7. The app receives the character and inserts it into the text. (On Windows, as a WM_CHAR message.)[R40]
  8. Font lookup: the font's character map (cmap table) maps the code to a glyph, the font's drawing of "A".[R43] Glyphs are outlines made of curves (quadratic Bézier curves in TrueType fonts).[R44]
  9. Rasterize: the outline is converted to pixels, lighting the pixels whose centers fall inside the shape. Anti-aliasing uses in-between shades on the edges so the letter looks smooth.[R45][R46]
  10. Framebuffer: the finished image of the whole screen sits in memory as a grid of pixel values.[R47]
  11. Refresh: the display redraws from that image many times a second, commonly 60 or 120 times per second (Hz).[R48]
  12. Light: each pixel is made of red, green and blue subpixels. In an LCD, a backlight shines through, and one thin-film transistor per pixel controls how much light gets through.[R49] In an OLED, each pixel makes its own light, controlled by at least two thin-film transistors, and black pixels switch fully off.[R50] So the last step is transistors too.
Background: ASCII and Unicode
  • ASCII was first published in 1963. It was a 7-bit code with 128 characters (33 control, 95 printable). It was revised in 1967.[R51]
  • Unicode extends this to every writing system. Version 18.0, released 16 September 2026, has 172,808 characters.[R52] Unicode's first 128 codes match ASCII, which is why "A" is 65 in both.[R41][R42]

D9

Is my microwave different?

Mostly the same logic, just far fewer transistors

Microcontrollers

A whole small computer on one chip. TI's 4-bit TMS1000 (1974, about $2 in volume) went into toys like the Speak & Spell. Microcontrollers now control microwave ovens and are found in large numbers in appliances and cars.[R67]

8-bit microcontrollers

Run washing machines, thermostats, smoke detectors and toasters.[R68]

Cars

Many modern cars have upwards of 100 electronic control units.[R70]

Apollo Guidance Computer

Built in the 1960s from thousands of simple chips, each holding 3-input NOR gates.[R72][R73] A real-world "build everything from one gate" machine.

Genuinely different

Mechanical thermostat

A bimetallic strip (two metals that expand at different rates) curls as the temperature changes and physically touches a contact to switch the furnace on. The strip is both the sensor and the switch. There are no transistors and no logic.[R69]

Analog electronics

Signals vary continuously (like a dimmer) instead of jumping between two values. Two-state (digital) operation is "far more reliable" than many-valued analog operation.[R71]

D10

Quantum computing: a different kind of bit

What a qubit is

  • Like a bit, a qubit can be 0 or 1. It can also be in a superposition, described by two numbers called amplitudes, one for 0 and one for 1.[R74][R75]
  • Amplitudes are not probabilities. They can be negative (and, more generally, complex numbers).[R75][R76]
  • Measuring gives one ordinary result, 0 or 1. If you just put a qubit register into an equal superposition of all answers and measure it, you get a random answer.[R75]

Interference: the engine

  • Amplitudes combine like waves. Some reinforce and some cancel.[R74][R76]
  • A quantum algorithm is a carefully choreographed sequence that makes the amplitudes of wrong answers cancel and the right answer's reinforce.[R75]

What this leaves outThis is one qubit (no entanglement), and ordinary light waves interfere the same way, so it shows the amplitude rule, not where speedup comes from. Real amplitudes are complex numbers (phases). The photon is never split: it's always detected whole, at one place.[R93]

Entanglement

  • Entangled qubits have linked outcomes: measuring one tells you about the others.[R74]
  • It is "correlation without communication". It can't be used to send messages faster than light.[R77][R78]
Myth: "A quantum computer tries every answer at once."
  • False. "Quantum computers won't solve hard problems instantly by just trying all solutions in parallel" (Scott Aaronson, quantum computing researcher).[R79]
  • He calls the "both 0 and 1 at the same time" framing "the fundamental misstep of quantum computing popularization."[R75]
  • The power comes from interference, and it only works for problems with the right mathematical structure.

What it's good for, and not

  • Strongest case

    Simulating molecules and materialsProbably the most important known application.[R76][R74]

  • Yes, in future

    Factoring large numbersShor's algorithm (1994) would break today's RSA encryption.[R75]

  • Small gain

    Searching unsorted dataGrover's algorithm: √N steps instead of N, provably the best possible.[R81]

  • Possibly

    Optimization and machine learning"Possibly" (IBM). Experts expect help "only modestly, if at all" for many problems.[R74][R75]

  • No

    Everyday computingEmail, games, browsing: classical computers stay the best tool.[R74]
